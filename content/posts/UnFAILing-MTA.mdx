New York City's public transportation infrastructure is frankly one of the best and most convenient in the world. It may show its age in much of its construction, dirt in most of its stations, and have need of some temperature regulation in Augusts, but it does provide access from any part of the city to any other part for $2, twenty-four hours a day, without need to worry about which "zone" you start or end your journey, or what time you want to leave for home. It even offers free transfers between Subway and bus service.

It is all managed by an [organization called the MTA](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Transportation_Authority_%28New_York%29) (Metropolitan Transportation Authority). Divisions of the same organization also run the Long Island Railroad, Metro North, the Port Authority station, the city's tunnels and bridges, and more.

In all, the organization manages to maintain a safe and reliable public transportation system.

As a New Yorker, I am always confident that the entire city is accessible to me, with either train, bus, or both. The reliability of New York's public transportation system (along with difficulty of parking in many areas, of course) is one of the primary reasons that so many New Yorkers don't own cars. I, myself, have not owned a car in 15 years.

This is relatively impressive, considering the scale and complexity of the organization's responsibilities. [From the WikiPedia article](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Transportation_Authority_%28New_York%29#Responsibilities_and_service_area):

> The MTA has the responsibility for developing and implementing a unified mass transportation policy for The New York metropolitan area, including all five boroughs of New York City, the suburban counties of Dutchess, Nassau, Orange, Putnam, Rockland, Suffolk and Westchester....
>
> [It] is the largest public transportation provider in the Western Hemisphere. Its agencies serve 14.6 million people spread over 5,000 square miles (13,000 km²) from New York City through southeastern New York State (including Long Island and the lower Hudson Valley), and Connecticut. MTA agencies now move more than 2.6 billion rail and bus customers a year and employ approximately 70,000 workers.

To be clear, the organization manages to deliver an impressive level of reliability despite its staggering complexity.

We New Yorkers gotta give them [props](http://www.urbandictionary.com/define.php?term=props) for that.

Yet despite all of this, there are still topics that consistently spur New Yorkers to curse the MTA.

I will be addressing the _second_ biggest [^1] recurrent gripe that New Yorkers have with the MTA: "unexpected" service alterations.

The biggest one, of course, is fare hikes. I'm not remotely familiar enough with all the topics that must be considered to explore this gripe, but any exploration of MTA gripes that omitted mention of fare hikes would simply be incomplete.

I do, however, have loads of experience and insight into communications infrastructures.

A big part of what makes this possible, naturally, is the city's bustling population. Subways and buses run 24 hours a day because New York is a town whose inhabitants actually take the opportunity travel around the clock in large enough numbers to make the operation practical.

MTA pretty solid job of getting New Yorkers around. needs to step up their game.

Expose service changes via RSS feeds.

Expose travel advisories via RSS feeds.

Expose train schedules via iCalendar.



[^1]: The biggest of the MTA gripes are, of course, [fare hikes](http://www.google.com/search?q=mta+fare+hike).
